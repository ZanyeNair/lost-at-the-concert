LOST AT THE CONCERT: MEASURING HOW RAFT RECOVERS ON UNRELIABLE NETWORKS
Talking points - Zayne - Nov 19, 2026


THE 30-SECOND VERSION
- Ever lost your friends at a concert with no signal? Everyone's texting different meetup spots and nobody knows which one's real.
- Computers have the exact same problem, and there's an algorithm called Raft that solves it. It's what keeps Kubernetes running.
- I built a simulator that shows Raft as friends at a concert, then broke it on purpose to see how it holds up.
- Big takeaway: bad signal never made Raft give a wrong answer, it just made it slower.


HOW RAFT WORKS (THE CONCERT VERSION)
- One friend is the captain who decides where to meet. Everyone else follows.
- The captain keeps sending "still here" texts. If you stop hearing from them, you wait a random bit, then ask the group "can I call the spot?"
- A plan only counts once more than half the group has it. That's the whole trick.
- Why half? Any two majorities always share at least one person, so a new captain always knows what was already decided.
- Here's how it maps:

    At the concert                                   In Raft
    -----------------------------------------------  ----------------------------------
    Captain who calls the meetup spot                 Leader
    "Still here" check-in texts                       Heartbeats
    Friend asks "Can I call the spot?"                Election timeout, then RequestVote
    Plan locked in once most friends have it          Entry committed by a majority
    Phone dies                                        Node crash
    Texts that fail                                   Packet loss
    Crowd surge splits the group                      Network partition
    Cut-off captain's plans thrown out afterward      Uncommitted entries overwritten

- This isn't just theory. Kubernetes, CockroachDB and a bunch of other real systems run on Raft.


WHAT I BUILT
- A web simulator where you can watch Raft happen live (it's on my GitHub).
- You can mess with it: kill the captain's phone, cause a crowd surge that splits the group, or make texts randomly fail.
- There's a toggle that flips everything from the concert story to the real technical terms.
- The experiment: crash the captain, then time how long until someone new takes over. Repeat 100 times for each setting.
- Making sure it's right: before trusting any numbers, I threw a ton of random chaos at it. It never ended up with two captains at once or friends disagreeing on a locked-in plan.


FINDING 1: BAD SIGNAL = SLOW REGROUP
(5 friends, 100 trials per level)

    Texts that fail   Median time to new captain   90th percentile   Recovered within 60 s
    ---------------   --------------------------   ---------------   ---------------------
    0%                2.7 s                        4.5 s             100%
    10%               2.9 s                        6.6 s             100%
    20%               3.2 s                        10.2 s            100%
    30%               5.4 s                        14.5 s            100%
    40%               7.3 s                        24.4 s            100%
    50%               12.9 s                       40.8 s            96%
    60%               24.0 s                       47.7 s            76%

- Up to about 20% of texts failing, Raft basically doesn't care. A new captain in about 3 seconds.
- After that it falls off a cliff. At 40% failed texts it's 7 seconds, and at 60% it's 24.
- And at 60%, about 1 in 4 groups never picked a new captain within a minute.
- So if you're building something on a bad network (like a drone swarm on spotty radio), you have to plan for this.


FINDING 2: IS A BIGGER FRIEND GROUP BETTER?
(Median time to new captain, 100 trials each)

    Texts that fail   3 friends   5 friends   7 friends
    ---------------   ---------   ---------   ---------
    0%                3.0 s       2.7 s       2.6 s
    20%               4.7 s       3.2 s       3.0 s
    40%               6.1 s       7.3 s       11.0 s

- Good signal: size doesn't really matter. Everyone regroups in under 3 seconds.
- A little bad signal: the group of 3 is actually the slowest. One failed text can block a majority when there's only 3 of you.
- Really bad signal: the group of 7 is the slowest (11 s vs 6 s), because the new captain needs 4 replies to get through.
- The trade-off is that bigger groups can lose more phones and still work:

    Group size   Majority needed   Phones that can die   Messages per check-in round
    ----------   ---------------   -------------------   ---------------------------
    3            2                 1                     4
    5            3                 2                     8
    7            4                 3                     12

- That's why real systems almost always use 5. It survives 2 failures without being too slow.


FINDING 3: EVERYONE CAN'T LOSE PATIENCE AT THE SAME TIME
If 3 friends all ask "can I call the spot?" at the same moment, the votes split, nobody wins, and they try again. Raft avoids this by making everyone's patience a little random. I tested how random it needs to be:

    Patience range         Width   Recovered within 60 s   Median recovery   Needed a re-vote
    --------------------   -----   ---------------------   ---------------   ----------------
    1.5-1.6 s              0.1 s   35%                     27.1 s            100%
    1.5-2.0 s              0.5 s   100%                    4.9 s             67%
    1.5-3.0 s (default)    1.5 s   100%                    2.7 s             14%
    1.5-4.5 s              3.0 s   100%                    2.9 s             9%

- Almost no randomness (0.1 s) was a disaster. Two-thirds of groups never picked a captain.
- A 1.5-second range fixed it completely.
- The lesson: the randomness has to be bigger than how long texts take to arrive, or people talk over each other.


WHY THIS MATTERS
- Slow is fine, wrong is not. For a bank or a hospital, waiting a few extra seconds is okay. Two servers disagreeing about your money or your medication dose is not.
- No human needed. When the captain goes down, the group fixes itself.
- The crowd-surge demo is the coolest part: the cut-off captain keeps making plans, but they never lock in. When the crowd clears, those plans get thrown out and nobody goes to the wrong spot.
- Where it shows up in real life: cloud servers (Kubernetes), databases, robot and drone swarms, hospital record systems.
- And honestly, every group project. Leader ghosts, nobody knows who's in charge, two people make conflicting plans. Same problem.
